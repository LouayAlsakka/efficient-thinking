Can a fixed AI model become more capable without becoming larger?

How much useful capability can a fixed intelligence get per unit of computation, and what moves that number?

Each paper holds the model fixed, measures capability with an external verifier, and changes one thing. The efficient-reasoning literature asks the same question from the compute side, by making a model think in fewer tokens; this series answers it from the experience side, with the weights frozen. Two methods on one axis.

Efficient Thinking3

I — Measuring What Capability Costs

Question
Does where you spend computation change what a fixed model can do?
Design
A 14 MB chess network with its evaluator fixed; search depth varied; strength measured in Elo against fixed opponents.
Result
About 650 Elo from search alone. Capability came from allocation, not size.
Ruled out
That a small model is simply weak: it was weak only when it was not allowed to search.
Handed on
The question VIII later asks: what can make that allocation better?

Paper I · closed, archived (DOI)

Efficient Thinking4

II — Where Search Pays and Where It Can't

Question
Does sampling a fixed model more and more keep improving the answer?
Design
Repeated sampling with and without an external verifier; the ceiling measured as samples grow.
Result
Sampling saturates at a consensus of the model's own errors; only an external verifier lifts the ceiling.
Ruled out
That more samples alone break the ceiling: the errors correlate, so consensus converges on them.
Handed on
VIII's experience is labelled by exactly such a verifier: "verified history", never the model's opinion of its past.

Paper II · closed, archived (DOI)

Efficient Thinking5

III — Efficient Judging

Question
A verifier is not free. When is a model-based judge worth its compute?
Design
An LLM judge against free majority vote, across policy strengths and candidate-field sizes, with the judge's cost charged.
Result
The judge beats majority vote only for weak policies and small fields. How much judging you get per unit of compute is itself the capability.
Ruled out
That a judge is a free upgrade: charged honestly it often loses to the vote.
Handed on
VIII's compute accounting carries the verifier's cost because III showed it must.

Paper III · closed, archived (DOI); one prediction left unscored, recorded

Efficient Thinking6

VII — The Elicitation Gap

Question
How much does a model know that it does not say?
Design
A probe on the hidden state against a judge that is made to answer under a pre-registered forced-choice definition, at four model sizes from 1.5B to 32B.
Result
The claim failed at every size. The forced judge rises from 0.53 to 0.73 with scale while the probe at matched depth stays flat; the tie rate collapses from 99% to 21%. The apparent gap was abstention, and scale fixed the format, not the knowledge.
Ruled out
A large reserve of usable hidden capability: what the model can report is bounded by its state. Decodable is not the same as usable, and the series had already met that in VIII-g.
Handed on
The hinge of the series: nothing is free inside a fixed model, so any gain at fixed intelligence must come from outside the weights. The state can be read directly; VIII reads it.

Paper VII · draft v0.5, E-E complete at four sizes. Forced-judge accuracy; the probe at matched depth is flat across the same sizes.

Efficient Thinking7

VIII — Experience Priors (the setup)

Question
Can a frozen model use its own verified past to search better, without changing a weight?
Design
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, bf16, weights never changed. A read-only linear head on one hidden layer at one decision, fitted on the model's own earlier episodes labelled by the test suite. 300 debugging problems, paired per problem, compute charged per episode.
Result
Base given the head's compute 22.3%; the head 31.7% (+9.3 [+3.7, +15.0]). At equal quality (24.0% both) the head spends 2.32× less compute. Transfers to a second search structure with the head re-fitted.
Ruled out
That the gain is the prompt: experience is carried in a read-only channel outside the prompt. That it is the model: not one parameter changed.
Handed on
A frontier that moves. VIII-b asks whether it keeps moving; VIII-c asks where the experience should come from.

Paper VIII §7.3–§7.7 · closed, reproduced on a second machine (+8.0 [+3.0, +13.3] against the published +9.3)

Efficient Thinking8

VIII-b — Does Experience Accumulate?

Question
Does a second generation of the agent's own experience keep improving it?
Design
The same loop run again on its own output; the gain measured against the loop's run-to-run noise, replicate against replicate.
Result
A small, consistent gain that does not separate from the loop's own noise. Duplication revealed the saturation; disjoint problems showed duplication was not the cause.
Ruled out
That the plateau is exhaustion of the model's ability to use experience.
Handed on
The limit is the region of state space the policy keeps returning to. VIII-c asks what happens if the experience comes from somewhere else.

Paper VIII-b · draft v0.3, every registered arm run

Efficient Thinking9

VIII-c — Where the Experience Is Acquired (the finding)

Question
Does it matter where experience comes from, with the model and the head held fixed?
Design
A head of identical form fitted on three sources: the agent's own experience; states recorded from where a second agent stood; states from a forced first look before guessing. 300 unseen problems.
Result
Base 22.0 · own experience 26.8 · another agent's places 35.4 · forced first look 41.2. Nothing about the model or the head changed between the columns.
Ruled out
That the plateau of VIII-b was the model's limit: it was the acquisition policy's. The cheap proxy tracks amount of experience, not origin.
Handed on
The series' claim: a frozen agent's learning plateau can belong to acquisition alone. Scope stated: one model family and one task family for the acquisition effect.

Paper VIII-c v1.0.12 · closed, reproduced on a second machine within the pre-registered bar

Efficient Thinking10

VIII-d · VIII-e — Selecting and retrieving experience

Question
Does choosing which past states to fit on, or retrieving them into the prompt, do what the head does?
Design
VIII-d: three selection rules (coverage, readability, a mix-matched control) against a random draw from the same pool, five registered arms. VIII-e: prompt retrieval of past experience (k=1, matched bank) against the frozen head, fifteen games.
Result
No selection rule beats a random draw from the same pool (within the design's ~5-point resolution). Prompt retrieval adds nothing and costs 5.9% more input; the head beats it by 13 to 15 points in every game.
Ruled out
That the +3 to +8 seen from selection was selection: it was the pool. That retrieval is a cheap substitute for reading the state: it is a null, three families running.
Handed on
The value is in how experience is read and where it was acquired, not in which items are chosen or whether they are shown to the model.

VIII-d measured 2026-10-01/02 · VIII-e measured 2026-10-01; a 23-point offline advantage of one label converted to no in-loop advantage

Efficient Thinking11

VIII-f · VIII-i — Three model families, two precisions

Question
Is the head's use of experience a property of one model, or of the method?
Design
The same protocol on Llama-3.1-8B (bf16), on Yi-1.5-9B-Chat at 4-bit, and back on family 1 at 4-bit, each with its own head fitted from its own history; registered before compute, independent second reads.
Result
The head beats base on all three families: +14.3 [+7.7, +21.0] on a disjoint 300 for family 2; +21.3, +22.0, +23.0 in three games for family 3; +26.9 for family 1 at 4-bit. Retrieval against base is a null on all three.
Ruled out
That the result needs full precision, or one architecture. The verdict transfers across families; the size of the gain does not.
Handed on
What replicates is the head's use of experience. The acquisition effect is still one family's and is registered as the next control.

Paper VIII §7.4 · plan entries VIII-f (2026-10-03) and VIII-i (2026-10-05)

Efficient Thinking12

VIII-g · VIII-h — What the head can and cannot be

Question
Can the verifier's label steer the model's state directly? And which label makes the better head?
Design
VIII-g: the separability precondition the adaptive-steering literature names, measured at two layers. VIII-h: a verifier-label head against an agent's-own-pick head on nine disjoint task sets, criterion ruled symmetric before any count was read.
Result
VIII-g: negative at its first gate (d′ 0.74 and 0.77 against a bar of 2; less separable than the agent's own pick). VIII-h: the verifier-label head wins by +16.6 points, interval [+14.6, +18.5], nine of nine.
Ruled out
That decodable information is steerable information: within-task the label is not separable enough to steer. That the agent already knows what makes experience useful: it does not.
Handed on
Useful experience carries information the agent did not hold in its own behaviour. The head reads it; steering cannot.

VIII-g measured negative 2026-10-03 · VIII-h measured 2026-10-05, second read signed

Efficient Thinking13

The claim, the method, and where it works

The claim. When intelligence is held fixed, the value of experience depends strongly on how that experience was acquired, not merely on how much experience is stored or how it is retrieved.

The method. The model's parameters are its thinking and are never changed; everything else that can move search is varied one at a time and measured, so each gain is attributed to experience and not to a changed model.

Where it works. The head decides where the next inspection goes, which is where the next unit of computation goes: a learned search prior, measured on allocation — equal quality at 2.32× less compute. The chain is acquisition policy → experience → search policy → computation → outcome, with the intelligence fixed at every link.

Efficient Thinking14

What the method risks, measured

Changing parameters carries an unquantifiable cost: what the new parameters lose is not known until it is found. This series never changes one, so that cost is zero by construction.

Its own risk is stated: the head can steer search away from a place where the vanilla model would have succeeded. That is measured problem by problem as the base-only cell of a paired table, and published beside every headline with both denominators.

Across the closed arms: 1.2% to 12.2% of the task set; 11.7% to 59.8% of the problems the vanilla model itself solved. The regression does not track the headline gain: the largest gains carry the smallest regressions. A head is applied only within the family it was fitted on; outside it the vanilla model runs unchanged.
