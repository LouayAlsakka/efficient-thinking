Efficient Thinking

Can a fixed AI model become more capable without becoming larger?
Efficient Thinking measures how search, evaluation, feedback, experience, symmetry and representation change the useful capability obtained from a fixed model and a fixed compute budget.

Where this sits. The efficient-reasoning literature asks the same question and answers it from the compute side, by making a model think in fewer tokens. This series answers it from the experience side, with the weights frozen: what can a fixed intelligence be given to read, and how much does that buy. Two methods on one axis.

One question, from every angle: how much useful capability can a fixed intelligence get per unit of computation, and what moves that number? Each paper holds the model fixed, measures capability with an external verifier, and changes one thing: search, the evaluator, the judge, taste, feedback, labels, the internal ceiling, experience, symmetry, representation.

What each paper established, one sentence each:

  • I — search extracts what the evaluator already contains.
  • II — sampling saturates because errors correlate; only an external verifier lifts the ceiling.
  • III — how much judging you get per unit of compute is itself the capability.
  • VII — made to answer, the model says what its state knows; nothing is free inside.
  • VIII — verified history, read by a frozen head, moves the frontier.
  • VIII-b — a second generation of experience buys a small, consistent gain that does not separate from the loop's own noise; duplication revealed the saturation, and disjoint problems showed it was not the cause: the limit is the region of state space the policy keeps returning to (draft).
  • VIII-c — a frozen agent's experience plateau can arise from how it acquires experience, not from exhaustion of its ability to use it: the same head on states from where a second agent stood, or from a forced first look, restores gains the agent's own experience could not. v1.0.12 · md

How the series reaches VIII

Paper VIII is the finding, and it stands on four results that came before it; each supplies a premise VIII needs, and none of them is a stepping stone only.

  • I — capability can come from allocation, not size. A 14 MB chess net gains about 650 Elo from search alone, with the evaluator fixed: where computation is spent is itself a source of capability. VIII asks what can make that allocation better.
  • II — search saturates, and only an external verifier breaks the ceiling. Sampling a fixed model converges to a consensus of its own errors; an external verifier is the one thing that lifts it. VIII's experience is labelled by exactly such a verifier, which is why it is "verified history" and not the model's opinion of its past.
  • III — judging costs compute, and judging per unit of compute is itself the capability. A verifier is not free; an LLM judge beats majority vote only for weak policies and small fields. VIII's compute accounting carries the verifier's cost because III showed it must.
  • VII — made to answer, the model says what its state knows; nothing is free inside. Under a forced-choice definition the elicitation claim failed at every size, which is the result: what the model can report is bounded by its state, and the state can be read directly. VIII reads it.

VI (the label ceiling: self-play stops where its labels stop being true; measured, unwritten) is VIII-b's relative on the training side. IV, V, IX and X are registered designs and ideas and say so.

The finding so far — Paper VIII and its chain

The claim, in one sentence. When intelligence is held fixed, the value of experience depends strongly on how that experience was acquired, not merely on how much experience is stored or how it is retrieved. Each clause is a measured result: how it was acquired (VIII-c: the same head on states from a forced first look, 41.2%, against the agent's own experience, 26.8%, and base, 22.0%); how much is stored (VIII-b: a second generation of the agent's own experience buys a gain that does not separate from the loop's noise); how it is retrieved (VIII-d: no selection rule beats a random draw from the same pool; VIII-e: prompt retrieval of it is a null, while the read-only head gains 13 to 15 points). Scope, stated so it cannot be taken away: the acquisition result is measured on one model family and one task family; what replicates across three families is the head's use of experience, not yet the acquisition effect.

What the series claims. This series separates the intelligence of a frozen model from the experience it can use and the experience it can acquire, shows each is measurable on its own, and finds that a learning plateau can belong to acquisition alone. The intelligence is held fixed by construction in every paper; experience is carried in a read-only channel outside the prompt; capability is verified externally and charged per unit of computation. The separation is demonstrated in one experimental system (VIII, VIII-b, VIII-c); whether it holds elsewhere is the question the reproduction route below exists to answer.

In plain words. A model that is never retrained can get better at a task by keeping a verified record of what it did before and reading it at each decision. Learning from its own past runs out quickly; learning from where another agent stood, or from a forced first look, goes further and restores the gain. The series measures each of these separately, on one system, and publishes the commands to reproduce them.

The method, in one sentence. This series treats the model's parameters as its thinking and never changes them; everything else that can move search — what the model is given to read, where that experience is collected, how it is applied at a decision — is varied one at a time and measured. Holding the thinking fixed is what lets each gain be attributed to experience rather than to a changed model, and it is what makes acquisition and use separable, measurable variables. The frozen-model-plus-readout half has neighbours (linear probes, contextual bandits over frozen states, hidden-state reward models; Paper VIII §6a). The separation of acquisition from use under a frozen intelligence, and the finding that acquisition is where the value moves, are this series' own.

Where in the chain this works. The head does not choose a final answer from information already computed; it decides where the next inspection goes, which is where the next unit of computation goes, and that changes what the system subsequently sees. In that sense it is a learned search prior, closer in kind to the selection rule of a tree search than to a learned action classifier, and its measured effect is on allocation: equal quality at 2.32× less compute (Paper VIII §7.6). The closest published readouts learn which action to take in the state the agent has encountered; this series learns how verified past experience should redirect future search, and VIII-c moves one step further up: the policy used to acquire that experience determines how useful the resulting search prior is. The chain the series measures is therefore acquisition policy → experience → search policy → computation → outcome, with the intelligence fixed at every link. Paper I showed that better-allocated search can substitute for model size; VIII asks whether accumulated, verified experience can make that allocation better without touching the model, and VIII-c asks what experience to collect so that it does.

What the method risks, and how that is measured. Changing a model's parameters to improve it carries a cost that is hard to quantify: every regression suite the model passed was passed by the old parameters, and what the new ones lose is not known until it is found. This series never changes a parameter, so that cost is zero by construction, and every suite the vanilla model passes it still passes (Paper VIII, registered prediction P5, holds by construction). The method has a risk of its own, and it is stated here because a reader should ask: the head chooses among candidates the model itself proposed, so it can steer search away from a place where the vanilla model would have succeeded. That loss is measurable, problem by problem, on the same problems: how many the base solves that the head does not. Paper VIII §7.6 reports it at the equal-quality point — 24.0% both, paired on 300 problems, 33 discordant each way — so at that budget the head's losses equal its wins and the gain is all in compute. Measured across the closed arms (2026-10-05, derived from the episode files, every published mean reproduced): the regression cell runs from 1.2% of problems (family 1 at 4-bit, 11 of 900) to 12.2% (one label on family 1 at bf16, 110 of 900), and it does not track the headline gain — the largest gains carry the smallest regressions. Against the stricter denominator, the problems the vanilla model itself solved, those same cells are 11.7% and 59.8%; both denominators are published, and the second is the one that prices the behaviour. Every arm publishes that four-cell table (both solve · head only · base only · neither) beside its headline, so the regression is a number on the page, never an inference. A head is fitted on one task family's verified history and, in every measured result, applied only within that family — the second search structure in Paper VIII got its own head, not a carried one — so the regression is bounded to the family where it is measured, and outside it the vanilla model runs unchanged. What a head does when applied outside its family has not been measured; it is registered as the next control, so that the per-family rule rests on a number rather than on caution.

The finding — Paper VIII-c

VIII is the setup; VIII-c is the finding. Same frozen model, same read-only head, 300 unseen problems. The base solves 22.0%. A head fitted on the agent's own experience reaches 26.8%, a gain that does not separate from base against the loop's own noise, and a second round of its own experience adds nothing separable (VIII-b). The same head fitted on experience recorded from where a second agent stood reaches 35.4%; fitted on experience from a forced first look before guessing, 41.2%. Nothing about the model or the head changed between those columns — only where the experience came from. That is the claim: a frozen agent's learning plateau can belong to acquisition alone. The head itself is not new: a small readout over a frozen model's hidden states, fitted on verified outcomes, is the same object as a value head or a learned verifier, and this series says so. What is new is what is held fixed and what is varied: the model and the head stay the same while only the source of the experience changes, which prompt-based memory cannot isolate, and which was not found in the value-function work read for the related-work pass.

The setup — Paper VIII

VIII — Experience, in three papers.

  • VIII · Utilization — verified experience improves a frozen model.
  • VIII-b · Saturation — more experience gathered by the same policy stops producing separable gains.
  • VIII-c · Acquisition — the plateau can arise from where experience is acquired; changing the acquisition policy restores useful gains.

Together, VIII to VIII-c separate three things usually called learning: fixed intelligence, the ability to use experience, and the ability to acquire experience worth using. Paper I began by asking how to spend an inference budget; VIII-c ends by asking how to spend an experience-acquisition budget.

Papers I–VII establish the frontier and its limits: search moves a fixed model along its quality–compute frontier, and only external information raises the ceiling. Paper VIII is the first paper in the series to move the frontier itself; VIII-c is what the series claims.

The setup, and the result to try to break in it. A frozen 7B model, given a read-only linear head fitted on its own verified history, solves more problems at the same inference compute — and, at equal quality, spends 2.3× less. The mechanism transfers to a second search structure with the head re-fitted. Whether a second generation of experience beats the first is being measured against the loop's own run-to-run noise, replicate against replicate, and is not claimed here. The headline reproduces from one command in about three hours on an Apple-silicon Mac.

Review

The challenge page: the claim, one command per result, the four places the author thinks each paper is weakest, and the terms of review.

There is no venue, no referee and no gate. Review is public and runs through the repository's issues. The four places the author thinks Paper VIII-c is weakest, so you know where to aim: the acquisition rule on a second task family (if "look before you commit" during collection does not move a head there, the claim is one family's); the exchange rate between forced-look and second-agent experience, measured in either direction; a held-out proxy that recovers the game ordering across the four heads, which would falsify the claim that the proxy tracks amount and not origin; and the noise floor, if the within-arm bar is shown too permissive or session drift larger than the base's within-day stability. Paper VIII's four are on the challenge page. A fatal flaw is worth more than praise, and the author will run any experiment you name.
