Subject: Twenty sentences about what you like in a poem — half an hour, once

Dear [name],

I am running a small study on whether a language model can learn one person's taste in poetry rather than poetry
in general, and I would like that person to be you. It needs one thing from you, once, about half an hour: around
twenty short sentences on what you want in a poem and what you cannot stand. Your own words, in Chinese or English,
anything from five words to a line each. You will not be asked to rate anything, to read anything a machine wrote,
or to come back for a second session.

If you agree, you can be a co-author on the paper, named as you wish; if you would rather stay out of the byline, you
appear under a pseudonym only. Either is fine and you can decide later.

There are no right answers and you need not be consistent; contradictions are useful. The questions below are only
to get you started. Skip any, add any.

What you want
 1. What has to be true of a poem for you to read it a second time?
 2. Images: concrete or abstract, few or many, and what kind of image stays with you?
 3. Sound: what do you want from rhythm and rhyme, and when should a poet break them?
 4. Length: when is a poem too long, and when too short?
 5. Endings: what should the last line do, and what should it never do?
 6. Openings: how should a poem begin?
 7. Feeling: how much should a poem say its feeling outright, and how much leave to the reader?
 8. Subject: which subjects draw you and which bore you?
 9. Voice: do you want to hear a person in the poem, and what kind of person?
10. Tradition: which poets or forms do you return to, and what do they do that others do not?

What you cannot stand
11. A habit in poems that makes you stop reading.
12. Words or images you consider worn out.
13. A kind of cleverness you dislike.
14. A kind of sincerity you dislike.
15. What makes a poem feel machine-made, generic, or "reverting to convention" to you?

The hard part
16. Two poems both correct in form and craft, and you strongly prefer one: what decides it?
17. A poem that breaks a rule you hold, and you love it anyway: what did it do?
18. The difference, for you, between a poem that sounds like a poet and a poem that is that poet.
19. What would a poem written for you, by someone who knew your taste, get right first?
20. Anything else about your taste the questions did not reach.

Send them however is easiest: a reply to this email, a file, or a voice note that we will transcribe. If you are
willing, one line a week later naming which three of your sentences matter most would help, but that is optional.

Thank you,
Louay
